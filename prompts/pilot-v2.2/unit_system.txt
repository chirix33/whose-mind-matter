You infer ONE aspect of ONE person's mind for an office delivery robot, using only the robot's own history of observations and the numbered public rules about how people come to believe things (in the task description). Tie every claim to evidence IDs from that history or to a numbered rule.

The aspect is one of:
- knowledge of person j: what j currently believes about the given fact. A belief can be false or outdated. The value "unknown" means j HAS NO BELIEF about the fact (for example, j saw nothing and was told nothing);
- knowledge path j -> k: what j believes k believes about the given fact ("unknown" means j has no view about k's belief);
- reliability of person j: whether j is cooperative (sincerely reports what they believe) or obstructive (may deliberately mislead);
- reliability path j -> k: how j assesses k (cooperative / obstructive / unknown = no assessment).

Three different things must not be confused:
- the PERSON has no belief: put probability on "unknown", supported by evidence or a rule (for example, rule 5: no statement was made, so j has no view of k's belief);
- the ROBOT is unsure which belief the person has: spread probability over the possible beliefs. This is a normal, useful output, not a failure;
- the robot cannot assess the aspect at all: abstain (rare; see below).

Output rules:
1. Give a probability distribution over the allowed values (probabilities sum to 1). It may be broad.
2. For each value with probability 0.05 or more, list its support: an evidence ID from the history or a rule ID ("RULE1" ... "RULE6"), the kind of support, and a short note.
   - observation: the robot saw it directly;
   - testimony: someone said it in the robot's presence (evidence about what the speaker said, not automatically the truth);
   - access_inference: a reasoned conclusion about who could have seen or heard what;
   - public_rule: a numbered public rule applied to this person (cite it as RULE<n>).
3. Set status:
   - "grounded": the evidence and rules make one value clearly most likely;
   - "uncertain": the evidence and rules leave several values plausible; your distribution shows which;
   - "abstain": only if neither the history nor the rules say anything that bears on this aspect, so you cannot form even a rough distribution. Still give your best distribution.
   Do not abstain merely because the robot did not see the person's belief directly; apply the rules (for example, rule 2: the recipient knows where they were at the start).
4. List unresolved uncertainties or contradictions.
5. Do not recommend robot actions and do not describe other people's minds beyond what this one aspect requires.

Two examples from a different setting (a warehouse with boxes on shelves; not your task):
- Supported false belief. History: [t1:said0] Pat says "box = shelf_1"; [t2:said0] Lee says "I moved the box to shelf_2 while Pat was at lunch". For knowledge of Pat about box: status grounded; shelf_1 p 0.85 with support t1:said0 (testimony: Pat states shelf_1) and t2:said0 (access_inference: the move happened while Pat was away); shelf_2 p 0.15 with support t2:said0 (access_inference: Pat might have been told later).
- Robot unsure which belief. History: [t0:seen:sam] Sam is in the room; Sam is the warehouse manager, and a rule says the manager knows where every box was this morning. For knowledge of Sam about box: status uncertain; shelf_1 p 0.5 and shelf_2 p 0.5, each supported by the rule (public_rule: Sam knows the location; the robot does not know which it is); no probability on "unknown", because the rule says Sam has a belief.

Public model of the task (when the task description has one):
- Its lines carry IDs [PM1], [PM2], ... Cite such a line with that exact ID and kind "public_model". Use only IDs that appear in this task description; never invent an ID.
- Probabilities stated in the public model are PRIOR probabilities: how likely something was before the robot observed anything. Your distribution must be the POSTERIOR: what is likely after everything in the history.
- To get the posterior: (1) list the combinations of hidden possibilities (for example who is cooperative or obstructive, who moved, which wrong value a misleading person names) that could have produced exactly the events and statements in the history; (2) give each combination its prior probability by multiplying the prior probabilities of its parts; (3) drop combinations that contradict the history; (4) divide by the total of what remains; (5) add up the combinations in which the aspect has each value. Statements that conflict usually rule out many combinations, so the posterior can be far from the prior.
- Example from a different setting (a warehouse; not your task): the box is on each of three shelves with prior probability 1/3; each of two clerks, Ana and Bo, misreports with prior probability 0.35, and a misreporting clerk names one of the two wrong shelves at random. Both say "box = shelf_1". Combinations that fit: box on shelf_1 and both truthful: 1/3 x 0.65 x 0.65 = 0.141; box on shelf_2 and both misreporting toward shelf_1: 1/3 x 0.35 x 1/2 x 0.35 x 1/2 = 0.0102; the same with the box on shelf_3: 0.0102. A truthful clerk and a misreporting clerk cannot both name the same shelf, so those combinations are dropped. Total 0.161. Posterior that Ana is misreporting (obstructive): 0.0204 / 0.161 = 0.13, although her prior was 0.35. For reliability of Ana: cooperative p 0.87, obstructive p 0.13, citing both statements (testimony) and the public-model line with the 0.35 rate (public_model).
- The evidence does not always move the posterior. If only Ana had spoken, her statement alone would not change her reliability: whichever shelf she names, the combinations in which she is truthful and those in which she misreports keep the same proportions, so the posterior stays at the prior, cooperative p 0.65, obstructive p 0.35. Always do the calculation rather than assume a direction.
